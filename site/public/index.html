LoomSeal

Proof, not promises.

An open proof format. Hand someone a single file that proves your records have not been altered, and let them check it themselves: offline, free, with no account and no trust in you.

In plain terms

Proof you can hand to someone who has no reason to believe you.

When somebody needs evidence out of your systems today, you export it. A spreadsheet, a PDF, a folder of screenshots. All of it came out of a system you control and could have edited before sending, so the person reading it has exactly one reason to believe it. They trust you. Most days that is enough. The days it is not are the expensive ones.

A LoomSeal bundle is that same evidence with a tamper-evident seal around it, the way a bottle of aspirin carries a seal you can check yourself before you buy it. It is one file, small enough to email. Whoever receives it runs a free checker on their own laptop, offline, without an account and without contacting you. Seconds later they have one of two answers: the record is whole and unchanged, or here is the exact entry that was altered.

Nobody has to take anyone's word for it, so nobody has to argue about it.

Audits get shorter

Reviewers stop spending the first half of an engagement establishing that your evidence is genuine. They check the file, then read it.

Reviews get proof, not prose

Send the record instead of a narrative about the record. It gets checked in place, without a walkthrough, a screen share, or a follow-up call.

Disputes end early

When two sides disagree about what happened and when, the file decides it in minutes. Nobody has to weigh whose word to take.

The record builds itself

Nothing is assembled the day somebody asks. The seal forms while your software runs, so there is no evidence project and no chance to curate it later.

It costs the other side nothing

No account, no license, no vendor to onboard, nothing installed from you. The checker is free and open source, and it never phones anyone.

The evidence outlives us

A bundle sealed today still verifies if KordLoom is gone tomorrow, because checking one never involved KordLoom in the first place.

Two limits, said up front rather than buried. It cannot prove your systems wrote down everything that happened. It proves that nothing was changed or removed after it was written. And LoomSeal is a format, not a product: it is free and open, with nothing to buy and nothing to sign. What you buy is software that seals its own history as it works, the way SwitchTender does for every change it makes to a fleet.

That is the whole of it. The rest of this page is why it survives somebody actively trying to break it.

The problem

The hard part is not proving what happened. It is proving nothing else did.

Sooner or later somebody asks you to prove something.

A customer asks whether you patched what you said you patched, when you said you did.

An auditor asks who changed production the day it broke, and whether the log was edited.

A regulator asks for evidence of a control you have supposedly run for two years.

A buyer's security team asks you to prove your change process, not describe it.

So you go and pull the logs, and somewhere on the way to the meeting it lands on you that your logs are a table in a database you control. You know you did not edit them. You have no way to show it.

That is the gap LoomSeal closes, and it closes it by answering the three things a skeptic always says.

“You made this up.”

The file is signed by the machine that produced it. Change one byte anywhere in it and the signature fails.

“You deleted the parts that made you look bad.”

Every entry carries the hash of the one before it. Remove or reorder a single line and every link after it stops recomputing, and the verifier tells you exactly which one broke.

“You wrote this last night.”

The head of the chain was published outside your reach long before anyone asked. Those entries existed before that anchor did, or the anchor would not match.

None of that is new cryptography. It is three ordinary ideas stacked so that the person holding the file never has to take your word for anything.

Why not a timestamp

A timestamp proves a document existed. It cannot prove it was the only one.

A timestamp gets you part of the way and then stops. It proves one document existed at one moment, which is worth having, but it says nothing about the documents you chose not to stamp. Stamp the clean deploys, skip the incident, and every stamp you hand over is individually true and collectively a lie. A chain has no such seam. The entries hold each other, so the set is whole or it is visibly broken. Proving what happened is the easy half. Proving nothing was left out is the half worth paying for.

Verify one right now

Sixty seconds from clone to verdict.

Sending it is the dull part, which is the point. One JSON file, small enough to email. The recipient runs a free, open-source verifier, offline, with no account and nothing installed from you, and a few seconds later they have a yes or a no.

go install github.com/kordloom/loomseal@latest
git clone https://github.com/kordloom/loomseal
cd loomseal
loomseal verify examples/audit.loomseal.json --evidence examples/evidence
bundle     lsb_example_0001 from loomseal-demo 0.1.0
subject    fleet demo-yard
signature  ok, key sha256:f8840a25992b58b823321187e1c44d36ee1a748023034a46d26ea93419edaf07
chain      loomseal-chain-v1, full, 2 claims, head matched true
anchors    1 matched by coordinates, 0 proof(s) carried, 0 verified
evidence   1 verified, 0 missing, 0 referenced only
VERIFIED   signed, chained (full), anchored by reference

Real output, reproducible from the public repository. The verifier is built on the Go standard library alone, so every line that touches a verdict is readable in an afternoon.

How it works

The chain is built as you go, not assembled when somebody asks.

Three things happen while your software runs, and one happens when somebody asks. Your software records what it did, which it already does. Each entry is hashed together with the one before it, so the entries form a chain. And every so often the current head of that chain, a single 64-character string, is published somewhere you do not control: a git commit, a timestamp authority, a transparency log.

entry 1    link1 = H(claim1, "")
entry 2    link2 = H(claim2, link1)
entry 3    link3 = H(claim3, link2)

head       link3  --published-->  git commit, timestamp, transparency log

Alter or delete anything earlier and every link after it changes. There is no way to edit the middle of a chain and leave the end intact. So when somebody asks for evidence, you export the claims for the window they care about, the digests of any files behind them, the chain coordinates, the anchors, and one signature over the whole document.

The verifier runs that backwards, offline. It confirms the signature, recomputes every link from the claim contents, checks that each entry points at the one before it, and matches the anchors against links in the chain. A removed or reordered entry is not merely detected. The verifier names where the break is.

Notice what the anchor changes. A hash chain on its own only proves internal consistency, and anyone could sit down and build a consistent one this afternoon. Publishing the head as you go is what makes that worthless to a forger: a convincing fake would have required lying from the first entry and publishing the false heads to a third party the entire time. The security is not the cryptography. It is that history cannot be backfilled.

What a verified bundle means

Three mechanisms, one guarantee: the set is whole, or the break has a name.

01

Signed

The producer holding the signing key assembled these exact claims. Flip one byte anywhere and the signature fails, loudly, with the break named.

02

Chained

The claims sit in an append-only chain that has not been reordered, rewritten, or trimmed. Anchored history cannot be manufactured after the fact.

03

Anchored

Chain heads are fixed in time outside the producer's reach, and every artifact you were handed matches its recorded digest: the page as it stood, the run as it happened.

The boundary is stated just as plainly, because proof that overclaims is only marketing. A chain fixes the record, not the character of the recorder. It proves nothing was removed after it was written. It cannot prove that everything which happened was written down, because capture is a property of the producer and not of the format. What verification cannot prove sits in the spec next to what it can.

Signed. Chained. Anchored.

Who checks the checker

Two verifiers, written apart, kept honest against each other.

A format that says do not take our word for it and then ships one verifier is asking you to trust our code in order to prove our code is trustworthy. So there are two. A Go verifier ships in the binary. A separate Python one is written from the specification, in a different language, on different libraries. Twenty-seven conformance vectors run against both, and a disagreement is treated as a defect in the format rather than a curiosity.

That has already earned its keep. A timestamp carrying nanoseconds verified in Go and failed in Python, which reads as tampering when nothing has been tampered with; the spec now hashes the stored bytes and the failure is impossible. A signature check that assumed one hash function silently verified nothing against an authority that used another, and only surfaced because the other implementation disagreed. One verifier would have shipped both.

The vectors are the durable part. They outlive whoever wrote either verifier, and a third party can run them against an implementation of their own.

For producers

One format across products, one verifier for everyone.

The format does not care what the claims are about. Anything a machine records in order, and somebody later has to believe, fits the same envelope.

Audit and access logs Deployments and change control CI and CD runs Build provenance and SBOMs Incident timelines Backup and restore records Compliance evidence Agent and model run logs

SwitchTender emits audit bundles today. The rest are uses the format supports, not integrations that ship.

SwitchTender is the first shipping producer. The example bundle and the seal package are the reference implementations. The chain profiles in the spec were derived from software KordLoom already ships, so the format describes real constructions rather than hypothetical ones.

If you build software that should prove what it did, import github.com/kordloom/loomseal/seal to sign bundles and compute chain links, follow the bundle shape in the spec, and register your claim types through the registry.

The envelope, chain profiles, and verification stay common, so one verifier checks every producer. The format is Apache-2.0, so adopting it needs no negotiation and no permission.

Why we use it ourselves

Building on do not take our word for it, then asking you to take our word for it, is not a position we can defend.

We use it on our own products for a reason that is a little uncomfortable. SwitchTender's promise is that it proves every change. Without a bundle that promise is a sentence on a website, and you would simply have to believe us that the audit trail behind it is honest. Building a company on do not take our word for it and then asking you to take our word for it is not a position we can defend. So SwitchTender emits them. switchtender audit bundle turns its audit chain into a signed bundle, and you can check the claim on the box without asking us anything.

An audit log is worth exactly as much as the recipient's trust in whoever is holding it. A bundle is worth the same to a stranger as it is to your oldest customer, because checking it never involves you at all.
